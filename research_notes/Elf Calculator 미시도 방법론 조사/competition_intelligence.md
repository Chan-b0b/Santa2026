## 1. Kaggle discussion threads and public notebooks: techniques, scores, and what did not work

### Takeaway

The public frontier is a per-puzzle merge of many authors' programs and stood at 128,491 on 2026-11-24 05:21 UTC, 2,841 above the leader (125,650); the leader's method is not public. The public techniques that moved the score are a backward beam search over a two-step expression dictionary for construction, then joint re-pricing of shared helper values (recipe pools selected by CP-SAT or MILP, value-DAG large-neighbourhood search with crossover between programs), and almost every purely local rewrite was measured at zero gain.

### Inferences

- (Inference) Our 147,310 sits near where the cited independent line was on 2026-11-19 21:30 BJT (147,052). That line then fell about 7,800 by applying, in order, multiplicative-structure recovery, recipe selection across several generated programs, grouped helper deletion, a value-DAG LNS with crossover, and withhold-and-rebuild. Those are the measured steps most likely to transfer to our programs.
- (Inference) Our per-puzzle best-of merge keeps whole programs. The public equivalent of crossover works one level lower: pool every recipe `value = a op b` from all candidate programs of a puzzle, select a minimum acyclic recipe set that reaches all targets with CP-SAT or MILP, then schedule into 48 registers. We have not tried this, and it reuses candidates we already generate.
- (Inference) Our `v = q·b ± c` reduction search is the same family as the public backward beam, but the public version draws b from "thousands of catalog values" (a two-step dictionary over the live registers) and keeps a beam with a 50-setting portfolio. Dictionary width and beam diversity are the likely differences, not the identity itself.
- (Inference) About 16% of the public 134.5k total is the first target of each puzzle (about 21 instructions). Our bootstrap cost per puzzle is worth measuring against that figure before tuning anything else.
- (Inference) Our meet-in-the-middle from the register pool and LNS on the value DAG overlap the guide's zero-gain list when used per target. The reported gains come only when a helper is cut with all its consumers and each consumer is re-inserted at its original position with a pricing engine deeper than five instructions.
- (Inference) Registers are rarely the binding constraint at this score level: relaxing to 72 registers and re-allocation alone both gave nothing. Effort on register scheduling beyond a correct last-use allocator is unlikely to pay.
- (Inference) The public merge fell about 4,000 between 11-21 and 11-24, and the gap between it and the leader stayed between 2,800 and 5,900. Any private solver is competing against a public baseline that moves by hundreds per day.

## 2. Statements about puzzle generation, families, design tiers and whether the trivial lower bound is reachable

### Takeaway

No host statement about generation was found. Participants report that the practice file gives family, design tier and a lower bound equal to the trivial count but no generating trace; that the generator does not chain targets; that "shared" puzzles are clusters around a hidden base and at least some puzzles have targets of the form k·a·b + r over a handful of hidden integers. The trivial bound of 26,898 is not reachable overall: a proven bound of 37,992 exists, and 21 puzzles are proven optimal.

### Inferences

- (Inference) The family names describe how targets relate to each other, not a hidden short program per target. "multiplicative" targets look like k·a·b + r over a few hidden integers, "shared" targets like base + small offset, and "independent" targets have no detected relation. Recovering the hidden integers is the one structural method with a measured large effect (636 + 155 instructions at the 150k level), and it suits our factor-sharing code.
- (Inference) The designed lower bound in the practice file is the trivial count, so `design_tier` probably grades difficulty by size or family rather than by distance to a known optimum. Nothing read explains the tiers.
- (Inference) The trivial bound is reached only where every target is one instruction from values already present. The 16 puzzles proven optimal by 11-19 average 3.6 instructions and are most likely tiny-family puzzles; for puzzles with 8–13 targets the proven optima or bounds are about twice the target count. The trivial bound is therefore a poor yardstick for all but the smallest puzzles.
- (Inference) The proven bound of 37,992 leaves the leader at 3.3 times the bound. The guide's counting argument (about 5 instructions minimum for a 60-bit target, about 7 spent) suggests the remaining headroom per large target is small, so further gains should come from sharing and from the roughly 21-instruction bootstrap, not from shorter single-target chains.
- (Inference) Structure tests that failed (recurrences, shared offsets across clusters, cross-puzzle templates, rational target relations) can be dropped from our backlog.

## 4. Past Santa optimisation competitions (2022–2025): meta-methods of the top teams

### Takeaway

Across 2022–2025 the top teams combined a strong constructive seed with a search that maintains several diverse solutions and recombines them (genetic algorithms with a good crossover in 2022 and 2025, beam search in 2023 and, with de-duplication, in 2025, passing solutions between different algorithms in 2024), and used exact methods only on small sub-problems. Winners' Kaggle write-ups could not be read; findings come from winners' repositories and one slide deck.

### Cited Findings

- Santa 2022: four teams reached the best-known score and all four used a genetic algorithm; variants were GA with edge assembly crossover on the constrained TSP, tour merging plus a limited GA on tours collected early (74,075.706541), GA-EAX-restart with a penalty function, and Concorde plus a GA. The deck's author judges simulated annealing weak when runs last months and deep local optima must be escaped, and GA strong "when long runtimes are acceptable, nearness to the optimum matters, and a good crossover exists". [fetch-summary] — [slide deck on Santa 2022 and genetic algorithms](https://speakerdeck.com/nagiss/santatoahctoyi-chuan-de-arugorizumu)
- Santa 2023 (a top solution, rank not stated in the README): puzzles were decomposed into clusters solved independently with small building-block move sequences; beam search with widths of about 100,000; simulated annealing for parity alignment; hill climbing with kicks; bidirectional and breadth-first search for short building-block sequences; dynamic programming to merge cluster sequences; an existing optimal solver for 3×3×3 sub-problems; corrections inserted "at every possible time, not just at the end". [fetch-summary] — [wata-orz/santa2023_permutation_puzzle](https://github.com/wata-orz/santa2023_permutation_puzzle)
- The Santa 2023 winner's total was 53,770 moves against 1,220,590 for the default solution. [snippet] — [arXiv 2512.03549](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.03549)
- Santa 2024, first place (talk dated 2025-02-06; 1,514 teams): hill climbing by moving one word, turned into iterated local search with kicks, plus a depth-first exploration of moves that do not worsen the score much, plus a small neural network that filters out candidates unlikely to improve before the expensive evaluation; brute force only for the 10-word instance; more GPU meant an advantage (about 50 evaluations per second on one A100). [fetch-summary] — [slide deck on the Santa 2024 competition](https://speakerdeck.com/nagiss/santakonpenohua-wosakututo)
- Santa 2024, second place (one member's part): "we shuffled solutions back and forth between our various programs. Because we used different algorithms from each other, when one program got stuck in a local minima, it seemed like another one could improve the solution". The framework was a priority queue of nodes expanded by neighbourhood, with choices on how many improvements to seek per neighbourhood, how many worse nodes to keep, when to discard a stale lineage and when to kick; neighbourhoods were compared by how smoothly the score changes under them; scores were cached on disk; best solutions and all their neighbours were re-checked in the Kaggle environment. [file-part] — [dgrunberg/Santa-2024-public README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/dgrunberg/Santa-2024-public/HEAD/README.md)
- Santa 2025, first place (write-up titled "genetic algorithm and GPU relaxation"): an island-model GA with 16 islands, mating within and between nearby islands, and reset of islands that stop improving; individuals are allowed to overlap during search and are legalised in a separate final step; symmetric genotypes encode only half or a quarter of the items; large instances start from a tessellated "crystal" seed because "the GA struggles to find these solutions unaided"; odd sizes copy the inner part of the N−1 or N+1 solution; the full submission added "an additional 'exploit' step in which the best solutions from multiple runs for the same number of trees are combined in their own genetic algorithm", a float64 compression step, and "a few manually built crystal solutions (some of them taken from public notebooks)". [file-part, notebook text cells] — [jcottaar/packing notebook](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/jcottaar/packing/HEAD/santa-2025-1st-place-solution.ipynb); [jcottaar/packing readme](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/jcottaar/packing/HEAD/readme.md)
- Santa 2025, second place (final score 68.800801749630): "a high-density initial solution + large neighborhood search". The large neighbourhood is remove k items (k up to 4) chosen by a randomised walk on a proximity graph, loosen the layout, re-place, then tighten the whole layout with simulated annealing. It is driven by beam search, which drops near-duplicates using a bottleneck distance because "if we keep near-duplicates, the beam effectively collapses to width 1", and by iterated local search with an SA-like acceptance rule and resets to the best. Seeds for N±1 were made by adding or removing one item from the N solution. [file-part] — [terry-u16/santa2025 README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/terry-u16/santa2025/HEAD/README.md)
- Santa 2025, fifth place: improvement modes include inheriting the previous or next instance's solution by adding or deleting one random item and compressing, and symmetric variants using N−2 and N+2. [file-full] — [saharan/santa-2025 README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/saharan/santa-2025/HEAD/README.md)
- A 27th-place Santa 2025 team reported holding a gold position for a while before "other teams found better ideas faster in the final sprint". [snippet] — [participant post](https://ch.linkedin.com/in/afina-inina-257a241)

### Inferences

- (Inference) The recurring winning pattern is a population of structurally different solutions plus a crossover that preserves good shared structure. In our problem the crossover with evidence behind it is recipe-pool selection: union the recipes of several programs for one puzzle and select an acyclic subset with CP-SAT. Our pipeline has LNS on one program and a whole-program merge, but no population and no crossover.
- (Inference) De-duplication by structure matters as much as beam width. A per-puzzle archive should keep near-best programs that differ in their helper sets, not only the shortest one. The guide's zero-gain multi-start used one rebuilder on public near-ties; the gains reported elsewhere came from recombining different helper sets, which is consistent with the 2024 and 2025 experience that different algorithms unstick each other.
- (Inference) "Relax, then legalise" (2025 first place) has a direct analogue here, searching with more than 48 registers and compiling back. It has already been measured at zero gain for this competition, so the analogy should not be pursued.
- (Inference) "Inherit from the neighbouring instance" (2025) maps to cross-puzzle transfer, which was also measured negative here because start values and targets differ per puzzle. Within a puzzle, however, inheriting from a sub-problem (drop one to three targets, optimise, re-add) did produce gains, which is the same idea applied where instances really are neighbours.
- (Inference) Exact solvers earned their place on bounded sub-problems in every year (Concorde in 2022, an optimal 3×3×3 solver in 2023, brute force for 10 words in 2024). Here the bounded sub-problems are tiny puzzles (provable optimality), recipe selection over an enumerated pool, and fixed-boundary sub-graphs such as the 25-output example rebuilt in 28 steps.
- (Inference) With no hidden test set and automated public merging, the last week will likely be a race of public-merge refresh plus private deltas. A workflow that re-merges public releases and re-runs our best operators on the changed puzzles daily is a transferable tactic, subject to the rules.

### Gaps

- Kaggle write-up pages for all four years could not be read. The first-place write-ups of Santa 2023 and Santa 2025 are known only by title or through the authors' repositories.
- Compute scale is documented only qualitatively (months of runtime in 2022; GPU-bound evaluation in 2024; an RTX 5090 recommended for 2025 first place). No core-hour figures were found.
- No source describes how much of any winning margin came from pooling public solutions, or what last-week submission tactics winners used.

## 5. Contests and puzzles on shortest arithmetic programs

### Takeaway

The closest contest, Al Zimmermann's "Factorials" (2013), asked for the shortest +, −, × programs for n! with n from 13 to 37, but its final report could not be processed, so winners' techniques remain unverified here. Countdown solvers contribute only pruning rules for exhaustive search. Little transfers beyond what the earlier survey already covers.

### Cited Findings

- AZsPCs Factorials (ended 2013-04-20): for each n from 13 to 37, submit a straight-line program for n! with as few steps as possible; the sequence starts at 1 and each later element is the sum, difference or product of two earlier elements; "subtraction can go in either direction", so negative intermediates are legal (the worked example for 5! is 1, 2, 3, 6, −5, −30, 150, 120 in 7 steps). A solution's subscore is the best submitted step count for that n divided by its own; sharing code or specific solutions in the discussion group was forbidden. [fetch-summary] — [AZsPCs Factorials contest page](http://azspcs.com/Contest/Factorials)
- The last 42,687 characters of the contest's final report contain only integer chains ending in 24! through 37!, each heading followed by a number such as 15 or 16 that the page does not define, and a note that no noteworthy solutions arrived after the contest ended. [fetch-summary, partial] — [AZsPCs Factorials final report](http://azspcs.com/Contest/Factorials/FinalReport)
- A paper on the same problem "relied on an exhaustive computer search of the short length straight-line programs", completed a full search up to length 11, determined the complexity of n! for n ≤ 22 and gave bounds up to n = 46. [snippet] — [arXiv 1306.3091](https://arxiv.org/abs/1306.3091v4)
- Countdown numbers-game solvers search exhaustively and prune by treating expressions that differ only by commutativity or associativity as the same, by allowing a + b or a × b only when the first operand is at least the second, by skipping multiplication or division by 1, and by ignoring results that recreate an input. The six-number game has about 30,000,000 possible expressions. [snippet] — [Haskell countdown package](https://hackage.haskell.org/package/countdown-numbers-game); [python-list thread](https://www.python.org/pipermail/python-list/2008-January/626365.html); [solver notes](https://incoherency.co.uk/countdown/pages/how-numbers-solver-works); [Oxford admissions page](https://cs.ox.ac.uk/admissions/undergraduate/courses/countdown.html)
- A paper titled "The (Final) Countdown" claims a new algorithm that speeds up resolution about 20-fold. [snippet] — [arXiv 1502.05450](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1502.05450)

### Inferences

- (Inference) The Factorials contest differs from ours in three ways that block direct transfer: it starts from 1 only, allows negative and unbounded intermediates, and has a single highly structured target. Tricks that overshoot the target or pass through negative values are illegal here because every value must be positive and at most 1.0 to 5.0 times the largest target.
- (Inference) If the undefined numbers in the final report are step counts, programs of about 15–16 steps for 24!–37! show how far multiplicative structure compresses a target. That supports prioritising structure recovery in the multiplicative family over generic chains.
- (Inference) Countdown pruning rules are already implied by a "distinct positive values" canonical form in our exhaustive tables: order operands of commutative operations, never emit a value already present, drop identity operations. They are worth a check against our depth-limited enumerator but offer no new search idea.
