# Register-constrained evaluation of computation DAGs (pebbling) and scheduling a straight-line program under 48 registers

Research date: 2026-11-24. Scope: only material not already in `notes/research.md`.

Verification labels used on every claim:

- **[V]** verified by me against the source's full text (PDF downloaded and text-extracted, passage read).
- **[A]** only the abstract was verified.
- **[U]** unverified: taken from a search summary or from a secondary source that reports the result. Needs a read of the original before it is relied on.
- **[inference]** my own mapping onto the contest; no source states it.

Contest model in pebbling terms [inference, used throughout]: a register is a pebble, an instruction is one pebble placement, the score is pebbling *time* T, the hard limit is *space* S ≤ 48. Recomputing a value costs one instruction, the same as computing it the first time. Unlike the textbook game, (a) the DAG is not given, we choose it; (b) starting values cannot be re-placed for free once overwritten; (c) targets only have to be pebbled once ("visiting" semantics), which is exactly Savage's rule set.

## 1. Black pebbling and register allocation for DAGs: exact and heuristic algorithms, and what DAG sizes are solvable exactly

### Takeaway
Minimum-register ordering *without* recomputation (one-shot pebbling) is NP-hard and hard to approximate, but a 2023 CP-SAT model proved optimal orders for real DAGs of 48–511 nodes within two hours, so it is exactly solvable at our 100–200 node scale. Minimum-*time* scheduling *with* recomputation under a register budget is PSPACE-hard in general, yet practical solvers exist (SAT for pebbling from quantum compilation, MILP/CP "rematerialization" schedulers from deep learning) that handle 250–1000 nodes heuristically and tens of nodes to proven optimality.

### Cited Findings

**Model and rules**
- [V] Savage's pebble game: a pebble may be placed on an input at any time; on a non-input only if all immediate predecessors carry pebbles; "A pebble can be removed at any time"; "(Goal) Each output vertex must be pebbled at least once." Time = number of placements, space = maximum pebbles on the graph. Sliding a pebble from a predecessor onto the result is allowed and "reflects the design of CPUs that allow the result of a computation to be placed in a memory location holding an operand." — [Savage, Models of Computation, ch. 10, §10.1](https://cs.brown.edu/people/jsavage/book/pdfs/ModelsOfComputation_Chapter10.pdf)
- [V] Allowing the slide move saves exactly one pebble over the strict rule, but in the worst case only "at the cost of squaring the time needed to pebble the dag". — [van Emde Boas & van Leeuwen, "Move rules and trade-offs in the pebble game"](https://perso.ens-lyon.fr/loris.marchal/docs-data-aware/papers/pebble-shift.pdf)
- [A] Sethi 1975 "studies register allocation for straight line programs in the context of a set of graph games" and shows several variants are polynomial complete. — [Sethi, SIAM J. Comput. 4 (1975)](https://www.doi.org/10.1137/0204020), abstract via [University of Arizona record](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/complete-register-allocation-problems/)
- [V, as reported by a secondary source] "The problem of determining whether a general DAG can be executed with a given number of pebbles has been shown NP-hard by Sethi if no vertex is pebbled more than once (the general problem allowing recomputation ... has been proven Pspace complete). However, this problem has a polynomial complexity for tree-shaped graphs." — [Marchal, Sinnen & Vivien, INRIA RR-8082](https://arxiv.org/abs/1210.2580)

**Complexity and approximability**
- [V] One-shot pebbling ("each node can receive a pebble only once") is equivalent to Register Sufficiency and to min over topological orders of the maximum cut size; its decision problem is in NP, "whereas the unrestricted pebbling problems are PSPACE-complete". It has an O(√log n · log n) approximation and is SSE-hard to approximate within any constant factor "even in DAGs with a single sink and maximum in-degree 2". Forbidding recomputation "can cause a huge increase in the pebbling cost". — [Austrin, Pitassi & Wu, arXiv:1109.4910](https://arxiv.org/abs/1109.4910)
- [V] With recomputation allowed, the minimum number of pebbles on indegree-2 DAGs is PSPACE-hard to approximate within an additive n^(1/3−ε); there are indegree-2 families where a constant k pebbles force Ω(n^k) moves. — [Demaine & Liu, arXiv:1707.06343](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06343)
- [V] Any bounded-degree DAG on n vertices can be pebbled with O(n / log n) pebbles (Thm 10.7.1) and some need that many (Thm 10.8.1). Lengauer–Tarjan: there is a space threshold Θ(n / log log n) below which some graphs need superpolynomial time. — [Savage ch. 10, §10.6–10.8](https://cs.brown.edu/people/jsavage/book/pdfs/ModelsOfComputation_Chapter10.pdf)
- [V] Extreme trade-off: graph H_k with 2k²+5k−6 vertices needs at least k pebbles; with exactly k pebbles any strategy takes at least (k+1)!/2 steps, "whereas there exists a pebbling algorithm using k+1 pebbles that pebbles each vertex of H_k once." Savage's comment: "excessive minimization of the number of registers used by programs can be harmful!" — [Savage ch. 10, Thm 10.3.1](https://cs.brown.edu/people/jsavage/book/pdfs/ModelsOfComputation_Chapter10.pdf)
- [V] Same phenomenon for indegree ≤ 2: families pebbled in linear time with one pebble above the minimum but needing exponential time at the minimum. — [van Emde Boas & van Leeuwen, Thm D](https://perso.ens-lyon.fr/loris.marchal/docs-data-aware/papers/pebble-shift.pdf)
- [A] Weighted one-shot pebbling is strongly NP-complete even on out-trees; fixed-parameter algorithm 2^O(d log d)·poly(n) for series-parallel graphs with out-degree d; every graph has a "dominant schedule". — [Jin, Purohit, Svitkina, Vee & Wang, arXiv:2312.13526](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.13526)

**Exact and heuristic orderings without recomputation (one-shot, minimise peak live values)**
- [V] Sethi–Ullman labeling gives space-optimal code for trees; "The problem is NP-complete for expression DAGs! [Sethi'75]"; "There are expression DAGs for which a non-contiguous ordering exists that uses fewer registers than any contiguous ordering"; the labeling algorithm "can serve as a heuristic (but not as optimal algorithm) for DAGs". — [Kessler, lecture slides TDDC86](https://www.ida.liu.se/~chrke55/courses/ACC/PDF-2014/MinRegScheduling.pdf)
- [U] Kessler 1998: dynamic programming over partial schedules finds register-optimal schedules for expression DAGs up to about 50 nodes; before it, exact solutions were limited to about 20 nodes by O(n!) enumeration. — [Kessler, Computer Languages 24(1), 1998](https://www.ida.liu.se/~chrke55/bib/j4.html)
- [V] Govindarajan et al.: "instruction lineage" heuristic (a lineage is "a set of instructions that can definitely share a single register"; lineages are fused via a lineage interference graph) plus an ILP for the optimum; on 675 small DDGs the heuristic was optimal in 99.2% of cases; spill loads/stores fell by more than 50% in each SPEC95fp benchmark. — [Govindarajan, Yang, Amaral, Zhang & Gao, IEEE TC 52(1), 2003](https://jnamaral.github.io/CDOL/papers/GovindarajanIEEE-TC03.pdf)
- [V] Chen 2023, CP-SAT model for min-register scheduling: N×N Boolean schedule matrix x[i][k], per-row suffix-OR matrix, live-bit matrix l[i][k] = OR over uses j of (ox[j][k] − ox[i][k]), objective Z ≥ Σ_i l[i][k]·size_i for every step k; "only needs 3 N × N boolean variables and one integer". With Google OR-Tools on a 4-core desktop and a two-hour limit it proved optimality on 4437 GPU-shader DAGs, "the smallest DAG has 48 nodes, the largest DAG has 511 nodes, and the average is around 90 nodes"; "many large DAGs" were not solved in the limit and were excluded. The same paper says Govindarajan's ILP was reported only on DAGs with "median of 10 nodes ... arithmetic mean of 19 nodes". — [Chen, arXiv:2303.06855](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06855)
- [V] Same paper, heuristic quality against those optima: generic Sethi–Ullman list scheduling on DAGs is optimal in 623 of 4437 cases, ≥1.5× optimal in 1246, average ratio 1.437; adding the "RP-reduction" rule (schedule immediately any ready node that does not raise register pressure) gives 730 optimal, 1124 outliers, ratio 1.418; the final combination is "about 17% higher than the optimal minimum on average" with "near 6% cases" at ≥50% above. — [Chen, arXiv:2303.06855](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06855)
- [V] Ahn et al. (SERENITY): dynamic programming whose state is the "zero-indegree set" (signature of a partial schedule), divide-and-conquer on the graph (cost drops from |V|·2^|V| to |V|·2^(|V|/N) for N equal parts) and "adaptive soft budgeting" to prune; brute force "takes days to schedule DAG with merely 30 nodes"; average extra compile time under one minute; peak memory 1.68× lower than TensorFlow Lite. — [Ahn et al., arXiv:2003.02369](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.02369)
- [A] OLLA: joint ILP for tensor lifetime (ordering) and placement; average memory saving above 30% in a median time under 10 seconds. — [Steiner et al., arXiv:2210.12924](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.12924)
- [U] Coudert, Mazauric & Nisse: branch-and-bound for pathwidth / directed vertex separation; the 2014 version reports exact results for any graph under 60 nodes within about 10 minutes. — [HAL hal-01266496](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01266496)
- [V] Uezato schedules XOR straight-line programs with a pebble game "equivalent to the standard pebble game of Sethi", forbidding recomputation, notes the problem is intractable and uses a DFS/postorder heuristic; register *assignment* for a fixed straight-line order is polynomial. — [Uezato, arXiv:2108.02692](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.02692)

**Solvers that allow recomputation (minimise steps under a pebble budget)**
- [V] Meuli et al. (reversible game): Boolean p[v][i] = node v pebbled at step i; clauses for initial state (all unpebbled), final state (outputs pebbled, others not), move clauses ((p[v][i] ⊕ p[v][i+1]) → predecessors pebbled at i and i+1), and a cardinality constraint Σ_v p[v][i] ≤ P at each step; K is increased until satisfiable; Z3 is the solver. Benchmarks: DAGs of 12 to 1257 nodes (ISCAS c17–c7552 and arithmetic "H operator" designs of 59–881 nodes), 2-minute timeout: "average percentage reduction of pebbles = 52.77", "average multiplicative factor for the number of steps = 2.68"; "the number of variables of the SAT problem is proportional to n²". A straight-line program for Edwards-curve point addition was pebbled with 24, 20, 16, 12, 10 pebbles, giving 74 operations at 24 pebbles and 110 at 10. — [Meuli, Soeken, Roetteler, Bjørner & De Micheli, arXiv:1904.02121](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.02121)
- [V] Quist & Laarman (spooky pebbling, LMCS Dec 2025): PSPACE-completeness proof; incremental SAT (Z3) with parallel moves, a skip step when a call times out, then six heuristic post-optimisers because SAT output "seems usually not to be an optimal pebbling strategy"; heuristics improve on SAT-only by up to 39%; benchmarks ISCAS85 DAGs with 172–1089 vertices, limits of 2 minutes (six smallest) and 8 minutes (four largest); solver is open source. — [Quist & Laarman, arXiv:2401.10579](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10579)
- [A] Bhattacharjee et al.: a reversible-pebbling *heuristic* constrained by a qubit budget cuts qubits by up to 63.2% at the cost of more gates. — [Bhattacharjee, Soeken, Dutta, Chattopadhyay & De Micheli, ISMVL 2019](https://msoeken.github.io/papers/2019_ismvl_1.pdf)
- [V] Checkmate: MILP with R[t][i] (node i computed in stage t) and S[t][i] (value i retained into stage t), memory-accounting variables, O(|V||E|) variables and constraints; "frontier-advancing stages" fix a topological order so that node i is first computed in stage i (lower-triangular R, S). On a 17-node linear graph at budget 4, Gurobi took 9.4 hours on the unpartitioned MILP and 0.23 seconds on the partitioned one, same objective. VGG16 solves in seconds; for DenseNet161 "no feasible solution was found within one day"; a two-phase LP-rounding approximation is provided. Open source. — [Jain et al., arXiv:1910.02653](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02653)
- [V] Moccasin: CP-SAT model with "retention intervals" (each node gets up to C_v intervals, each starting with a (re)computation), O(n) integer variables instead of O(n²) Booleans, cumulative constraint for the memory budget; C_v = 2 "is often good enough"; up to an order of magnitude faster than Checkmate on 100–250 node graphs; on 500- and 1000-node graphs Checkmate "times out with no solution" in 3 hours while Moccasin "converges to a good solution ... under less than an hour". The formulation "does not require an input topological order" (Checkmate does), but all published experiments add that constraint to compare with Checkmate, so the free-order mode is untested in the paper. — [Bartan et al., arXiv:2304.14463](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14463)
- [V] Kumar et al.: recursive balanced-separator algorithm on a tree decomposition of width k gives schedule length O(T_onepass · k · n^log(k+2)) and memory O((M_in + k·M_max) log n); the paper says absolute memory-optimal rematerialization "is known to be PSPACE-complete". Their baseline "GreedyRemat" (used by XLA) rematerializes, when over budget, the already-scheduled operation that most reduces memory. — [Kumar, Purohit, Svitkina, Vee & Wang, NeurIPS 2019](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2019/file/ffe10334251de1dc98339d99ae4743ba-Paper.pdf)
- [V] Papp, Böhnlein & Yzelman 2025: ILP for red-blue pebbling with computation costs (time-indexed "has red pebble" variables, step merging); the full ILP is used on DAGs of 40–80 nodes with a 60-minute COPT limit, "typically not enough to run the solving process to optimality", and "already becomes untractable for DAGs with a few hundred nodes"; a divide-and-conquer variant on 264–464 nodes was 1.13× *worse* than the baseline on DAGs that do not partition well. For a single processor, "we can simply use a DFS as our first-stage scheduler ... the ILPs could only improve on this baseline in 2 out of the 15 instances". Forbidding recomputation raised cost by "up to a 1.4×" on some instances. — [Papp, Böhnlein & Yzelman, arXiv:2507.17411](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.17411)
- [V] Red-blue pebbling is NP-hard in all studied variants (including one where each computation has a cost) and greedy strategies can be far from optimal. — [Papp & Wattenhofer, arXiv:2005.08609](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.08609)

## 2. Time–space trade-offs for addition chains and exponentiation with limited memory; is there a quantitative register-versus-length relation?

### Takeaway
The addition-chain literature gives only a coarse relation: a window of k bits needs about 2^(k−1) stored values and saves roughly λ/2 − λ/(k+1) operations on a λ-bit exponent, and Lucas-type chains run with three stored values at 1.5–2 operations per bit. Nobody states a theorem linking register count to chain length ("cannot be summarized in a simple formula", Sauerbrey & Dietel), and I found nothing at all for programs with multiplication and many targets, so the cost of our 48-register cap has to be estimated by counting, not looked up.

### Cited Findings
- [V] Sauerbrey & Dietel compare exponentiation rules "with regard to the number of multiplications and the amount of memory required"; the hardware model is a fixed bank of registers R_i with a controller choosing source and destination per step. — [Sauerbrey & Dietel, EUROCRYPT '92](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/3-540-47555-9_15.pdf)
- [V] Their operation count: (length of exponent) − (length of MSB window) + (number of non-zero windows − 1) + (length of the addition sequence for the window values − 1). — [same](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/3-540-47555-9_15.pdf)
- [V] "The effects of the computation rule on the required number of registers, cannot be summarized in a simple formula". Four factors are named: non-adjacent windows sharing a value (it must be stored until reused); a window value needed to build later window values; by-products of building one window value needed later; and how well windows are sorted by increasing value from left to right (a value can be dropped once its row is started and it is not needed again). — [same](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/3-540-47555-9_15.pdf)
- [V] "for 512 bit exponents the standard binary algorithm leads on the average to 767 operations and 2 required registers." For the window methods, "window sizes leading to good results for the number of operations require a lot of registers, and vice versa." Register demand first rises steeply with window size (many distinct values recur and must be kept), then falls again for very large windows because values stop recurring. — [same](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/3-540-47555-9_15.pdf)
- [V] Their remedy, "size oriented window distribution": choose windows so that window values strictly increase from left to right, so each value appears once and in sorted order; reported as "a better compromise between operation count and memory demand". — [same](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/3-540-47555-9_15.pdf)
- [V] Coster's report compares 12 addition-chain / sequence / vector-chain algorithms "on two criteria namely the length of the chains/sequences and the memory usage", in an accumulator machine with LD/STO/ADD/DBL. Results: the binary algorithm is the only one that uses one memory location; "If we use 2 memory locations then the length of the chain is in general much smaller"; k-bit window algorithms need 2^(k−1) locations for the odd values 1, 3, …, 2^k − 1, plus one more in the variant that needs a doubling register; the generalized continued-fraction algorithm produces a vector addition chain of width n with n locations "and this is optimal"; some algorithms are "ill conditioned" (memory grows with the size of the numbers). — [Coster, CWI Report CS-R9024, 1990](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/5726/5726D.pdf)
- [V] Lucas chains (each new element needs the difference of its two summands to be in the chain): Montgomery's continued-fraction algorithm "maintains A = X_a(P), B = X_b(P), and C = X_{a−b}(P)" at all times, i.e. three stored values. The binary Lucas method satisfies L_b(n) ≤ 2 lg n and "costs 30% more than the lower bound predicts"; CFRC is within 2% of the lower bound on average for primes below 10^4 but its worst case is not O(log n); PRAC is within 8% on average for primes below 10^6 and never costs more than 4 log2 n. — [Montgomery, "Evaluating recurrences of form X_{m+n} = f(X_m, X_n, X_{m−n}) via Lucas chains", 1983/1992](https://cr.yp.to/bib/1992/montgomery-lucas.pdf)
- [U] Differential addition chains: CFRC needs about 1.5 additions per bit versus 2 for the ladder; the best known lower bound is about 1.44 per bit; PRAC chains "use more complicated data flow and more storage than continued-fraction chains". — [IACR ePrint 2024/1044](https://eprint.iacr.org/2024/1044.pdf)
- [U] Ben-Or & Cleve: any polynomial-size algebraic *formula* over a ring can be evaluated by a polynomial-length straight-line program using 3 registers. — [Ben-Or & Cleve, SIAM J. Comput. 21(1), 1992](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/computing-algebraic-formulas-using-a-constant-number-of-registers-14/)
- [U] The `addchain` tool has an allocation pass whose comment says it "assigns a minimal number of temporary variables to execute a program" for a fixed instruction order. — [addchain acc/pass/alloc.go (Debian source mirror)](https://sources.debian.org/src/addchain/0.4.0-2/acc/pass/alloc.go)
- [U] Leads not read: Brickell–Gordon–McCurley–Wilson on exponentiation with precomputation and storage ([paper](https://dmgordon.org/papers/fast.pdf)); a note by Herendi with a lower bound on the stored base set for k-step exponentiation ([paper](https://core.ac.uk/download/160935922.pdf)).

### Inferences
- For addition-only chains the relation is: R ≈ 2^(k−1) + 1 registers buy window size k, and length ≈ λ − k + λ/(k+1) + 2^(k−1) for a λ-bit target (Sauerbrey's count with the usual expected number of sliding windows). For λ ≤ 60 the best k is 3 or 4, needing 5–9 registers. So 48 registers never bind for a single addition chain, which agrees with the "at most 40 live values" measurement already in `notes/research.md`. The cap is not an addition-chain phenomenon. [inference]
- The transferable idea from Sauerbrey & Dietel is the *ordering* principle, not the numbers: arrange the work so that reusable values are consumed in a sorted sweep and each can be dropped after its last consumer. For us: order targets so that targets sharing an intermediate are adjacent in the schedule, which shortens the intermediate's live range. [inference]
- Lucas chains and ladders are the extreme low-memory end (3 values, 1.5–2 operations per bit). At our cost level (about 5 instructions per target in total) they are an order of magnitude too long and give no usable trade-off point. [inference]
- Ben-Or & Cleve cannot be used: the construction needs subtraction through zero/negative intermediate values and exponential-in-depth length; both violate "positive integer ≤ value_limit" and our length budget. [inference]
- Counting estimate of what pool size is worth, in the style of the counting bound of `notes/research.md` §4.3 but with a pool [inference, my own arithmetic, not from a source]. A program that builds one new value from a pool of P values with k instructions, using each intermediate once, is a binary formula with k+1 pool leaves: at most W(k+1)·4^k·P^(k+1) values, W = number of unordered binary tree shapes (1, 1, 2, 3, 6, 11 for 2–7 leaves). In bits:

  | k instructions | P = 48 | P = 96 | P = 170 |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 3 | 29.3 | 33.3 | 36.6 |
  | 4 | 37.5 | 42.5 | 46.6 |
  | 5 | 46.1 | 52.1 | 57.0 |
  | 6 | 54.6 | 61.6 | 67.3 |

  Reading: from 48 pool values, a generic 49-bit target cannot be reached in 4 instructions and at most about one in eight can be reached in 5; from 170 pool values 5 always suffices by count and a minority could be reached in 4. So for *unrelated* targets the cap costs at most about one instruction per target by counting (5.3 vs 4.2 if interpolated), and each doubling of the pool is worth at most (k+1) ≈ 6–7 bits ≈ 0.7–0.8 instruction per target. One register slot out of 48 is then worth at most about 0.02–0.03 instruction per target, or roughly 0.7 instruction per 30-target puzzle. These are upper bounds on the benefit: real search is well above the counting bound (our measured 6.3 with the whole value set as pool versus 4.2–4.8 by count), and they assume generic targets.
- The same table says the leaderboard figure of 4.67 per target is below what unrelated 49-bit targets allow from a 48-value pool, so top solutions must rely on targets sharing structure (several targets one instruction away from common intermediates). The register question that matters is therefore how many *shared* intermediates can be kept alive across the targets that use them. [inference]

### Gaps
- No theorem or experiment relating register count to program length for straight-line programs with multiplication, exact division and multiple targets was found. The counting table above is my estimate, not a literature result.
- The figures of Sauerbrey & Dietel (operations and registers versus window size) are plots; the numeric values between "767 operations, 2 registers" and the minimum of the curves could not be extracted from the text.
- Coster's Lemma 3 was read from an OCR scan; the per-algorithm memory formulas beyond the window and binary cases are partly illegible.
- The differential-addition-chain paper (ePrint 2024/1044), Ben-Or & Cleve, Brickell et al. and Herendi were not read in full.

## 3. Compiler techniques: rematerialization, live-range splitting, spill-cost models, Belady-style eviction with known future use; which are provably optimal offline?

### Takeaway
With the whole program known, eviction is provably optimal in two cases: furthest-next-use (Belady/MIN) when every reload costs the same, and a min-cost-flow / totally unimodular ILP when reloads have different but fixed costs. It becomes NP-hard as soon as costs depend on state (dirty pages/stores, live ranges with holes), which is also our situation once a recomputation needs operands that were themselves evicted.

### Cited Findings
- [V] Theorem 1 (Furthest First): on a basic block (interval graph), unweighted spill-everywhere is solved optimally by Belady's greedy furthest-use rule. Theorem 2: the *weighted* version is also polynomial, because the constraint matrix of the ILP is the incidence matrix of an interval hypergraph and is totally unimodular; "It is less known" than Belady. Variants with holes in the live ranges are NP-complete even with unit weights and on a basic block (Theorems 10, 11). — [Bouchez, Darte & Rastello, "On the Complexity of Spill Everywhere under SSA Form", arXiv:0710.3642](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.3642)
- [V] Paging without write-backs "can be optimally solved by the furthest-first rule". Local register allocation with load *and* store costs is NP-hard, already for off-line paging with write-backs. Weighted off-line caching (each item has its own reload cost, no stores) is "much simpler ... because the costs do not depend on the page status" and is solved as a minimum-cost network flow. A 2-approximation (algorithm W) exists for the general problem; "conservative furthest-first" (CFF: among the values requested furthest in the future, evict one that is not alive) returned solutions of comparable cost to W on average and was much faster. A branch-and-bound based on the flow relaxation "never ran for more than nine minutes even on long basic blocks and with 256 registers". — [Farach & Liberatore, "On Local Register Allocation", DIMACS TR 97-33](https://archive.dimacs.rutgers.edu/archive/TechnicalReports/abstracts/1997/97-33.html)
- [V] In a fixed compute order with unit-size values, "the best strategy is simply that whenever we need to evict a value from cache, we select the value which is not needed for the longest time (known as Bélády's optimal algorithm or Clairvoyant algorithm)"; "with general node weights [sizes], the problem immediately becomes NP-hard." — [Papp, Böhnlein & Yzelman, arXiv:2507.17411](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.17411)
- [V] The MIN algorithm "has often been applied to straight-line code"; Braun & Hack extend it to control-flow graphs and cut executed reloads by 54.5% versus a linear-scan allocator. For a single basic block the plain MIN algorithm is what they start from. — [Braun & Hack, CC 2009](https://pp.ipd.kit.edu/uploads/publikationen/braun09cc.pdf)
- [V] Rematerialization: "it can sometimes be beneficial to recompute (that is, rematerialize) a value to be reused rather than occupying a register until its later use or spilling the value." Goodwin & Wilken's 0-1 IP has explicit remat(t, r, p) variables; Appel & George decompose into optimal spilling by IP followed by assignment; Colombet et al.'s IP adds rematerialization and "around half of the estimated improvement over Appel and George's approach is due to supporting rematerialization". — [Castañeda Lozano & Schulte, survey, arXiv:1409.7628](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.7628)
- [U] Briggs, Cooper & Torczon introduced rematerialization in a Chaitin-style allocator: values that can be recomputed from operands that are always available are recomputed instead of being stored and reloaded. — [Briggs, Cooper & Torczon, PLDI 1992](https://bibtex.github.io/PLDI-1992-BriggsCT.html)
- [V] Register *assignment* is not the hard part for straight-line code: once the set of live values at each point is at most the register count, a valid assignment exists and is found in polynomial time (SSA / interval-graph property). — [Uezato, arXiv:2108.02692](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.02692); [Castañeda Lozano & Schulte, arXiv:1409.7628](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.7628)
- [V] Live-range splitting has a direct counterpart in rematerialization schedulers: Moccasin gives each value up to C_v separate retention intervals, each beginning with a recomputation, and reports that C_v = 2 is usually enough. — [Bartan et al., arXiv:2304.14463](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14463)
- [V] Online eviction score for rematerialization: DTR evicts the tensor minimising h(t) = c(t) / (m(t)·s(t)), where s is staleness, m is size and c is the recompute cost *including the evicted neighbourhood* (already-evicted values that would have to be recomputed too). Theorem 3.1: one forward and one backward pass over an N-node linear network runs in O(N) operations with Ω(√N) memory. Theorem 3.2: for any deterministic eviction heuristic there is a network where it needs Ω(N/B) times more computation than an optimal static plan that may *reorder* the work. — [Kirisame et al., arXiv:2006.09616](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.09616)

### Inferences
All [inference].

- Translation table. In the contest there is no memory to spill to: "spill" = overwrite, "reload" = recompute. A value whose two operands are guaranteed alive reloads at cost 1; otherwise the cost is recursive. So:
  1. If every evictable value could be recomputed in one instruction from values that are never evicted, Belady's rule (evict the value whose next use is furthest) is exactly optimal for a fixed instruction order.
  2. If each value has a fixed but different recompute cost (for example the size of its private cone above a permanently kept base set), the optimum for a fixed order is a min-cost flow, or equivalently "choose a maximum-weight set of live intervals with at most 48 overlapping at any time"; solvable in polynomial time, and at our size instantly.
  3. If recompute costs depend on what else was evicted, it is black pebbling with recomputation: no polynomial guarantee, use the solvers of section 1.
- What is new relative to what we tried. "Evict large and long-unused values first" is a size-plus-LRU rule; LRU looks backwards. The compiler result says the forward-looking rule is the optimal one when the future is known, and we can know it: run the construction once with no register limit, record for each pool value the instruction indices at which it is used as an operand, then replay with 48 registers using furthest-next-use (case 1) or min-cost flow (case 2). Values with no future use are freed immediately.
- Rematerialization candidates. Values that are one instruction away from permanently kept values (small constants such as 1, 2, powers of two built from a starting value, a starting value squared) are the analogue of "never-killed" values: do not keep them between distant uses, rebuild them. The break-even is simple: keeping v across a gap is worth it only if the slot it blocks would not save more than one instruction elsewhere during the gap. With the rough shadow price from section 2 (about 0.7 instruction per slot per whole puzzle) a 1-instruction value that is reused only once, far away, is nearly break-even, and a value reused two or more times should be kept.
- A DTR-style score adapted to known futures: evict the value minimising (recompute cost including evicted operands) / (distance to next use). It reduces to Belady when all costs are 1 and it penalises evicting a value whose operands are already gone.
- Expected benefit: on puzzles where the pool is saturated, replacing an LRU-like rule by a clairvoyant rule is the standard gap between LRU and MIN in caching; the size of that gap on our traces is unknown and must be measured. It is cheap to try (a replay pass, no new search).
- Reasons it might not transfer: (a) the future use trace comes from an unconstrained run; once evictions change which pool values are available, later construction choices change and the trace is no longer valid, so the replay must be iterated or interleaved with construction; (b) optimal eviction for a *fixed* order says nothing about choosing the order, and Theorem 3.2 of DTR shows order can matter by a factor N/B; (c) the store-cost and "holes" hardness results do not apply to us (no stores), but the dependent-cost case does.

### Gaps
- Briggs–Cooper–Torczon was not read (ACM paywall); the description above is from search summaries and the survey's general definition.
- I did not find a compiler paper that treats *offline optimal rematerialization on straight-line code with recursive recompute costs*; the closest are the deep-learning schedulers of section 1.
- No measurement exists of the LRU-versus-clairvoyant gap on traces like ours.

## 4. Pool management as an optimisation problem: which values to retain, and interleaving target order with retention

### Takeaway
I found no paper that poses "choose at most k live values to maximise what can be built next" for arithmetic programs. The nearest formal analogues are materialized-view selection (greedy with a 63% guarantee when benefit is monotone and has diminishing returns), offline weighted caching (exact by min-cost flow for a fixed request sequence) and peak-memory scheduling (order and retention solved jointly by ILP/CP); the main lesson from the last group is that solving order and retention separately can be far from optimal in theory but was close to optimal in the single-processor experiments.

### Cited Findings
- [U] Harinarayan, Rajaraman & Ullman: choose k views to materialize so that the total cost of answering all queries is minimal; greedy picks the view with the largest marginal benefit each round and achieves at least (e−1)/e ≈ 63% of the optimal benefit. — [summary of "Implementing Data Cubes Efficiently", SIGMOD 1996](https://mwhittaker.github.io/papers/html/harinarayan1996implementing.html); original at [Berkeley copy](https://dsf.berkeley.edu/cs286/papers/datacube-sigmod1996.pdf) (not retrievable, certificate error)
- [V] For a fixed request sequence and items of equal size but different fault costs, the optimal offline cache content over time is a min-cost network flow. — [Farach & Liberatore, DIMACS TR 97-33](https://archive.dimacs.rutgers.edu/archive/TechnicalReports/abstracts/1997/97-33.html)
- [V] Two-stage scheduling (first order the computation ignoring memory, then manage the cache) "can return a schedule which is a linear factor away from the actual optimum, even if both stages are optimal"; yet with one processor, a DFS order plus clairvoyant eviction was improved by the joint ILP in only 2 of 15 instances; with 4 processors the joint ILP reached 0.77× the baseline cost (geometric mean). — [Papp, Böhnlein & Yzelman, arXiv:2507.17411](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.17411)
- [V] Greedy pebbling rules can be much worse than optimal in the worst case. — [Papp & Wattenhofer, arXiv:2005.08609](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.08609); [Böhnlein, Papp & Yzelman, arXiv:2409.03898](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.03898) (Lemma 4)
- [V] Reordering matters more than the eviction rule in the worst case: any deterministic online heuristic can be Ω(N/B) worse than a static plan that reorders. — [Kirisame et al., arXiv:2006.09616](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.09616)
- [V] A DFS/postorder traversal is the standard low-memory order: it is the basis of the Sethi–Ullman labeling for trees, of Uezato's heuristic for straight-line programs, and of the strong single-processor baseline in Papp et al. — [Kessler slides](https://www.ida.liu.se/~chrke55/courses/ACC/PDF-2014/MinRegScheduling.pdf); [Uezato, arXiv:2108.02692](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.02692); [Papp, Böhnlein & Yzelman, arXiv:2507.17411](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.17411)
- [V] For trees, a memory-optimal traversal need not be a postorder; Liu gave an exact algorithm without the postorder restriction. — [Marchal, Sinnen & Vivien, arXiv:1210.2580](https://arxiv.org/abs/1210.2580) (reporting Liu)
- [V] Joint order-and-lifetime models at our scale exist: Chen's CP-SAT (order + live bits), OLLA's ILP (order + placement), SERENITY's DP over zero-indegree sets with divide-and-conquer. — [Chen, arXiv:2303.06855](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06855); [Steiner et al., arXiv:2210.12924](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.12924); [Ahn et al., arXiv:2003.02369](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.02369)
- [A] "Dominant schedules": for any computation graph there is a schedule of a subgraph that is best in every context, which allows composing schedules of independent parts. — [Jin et al., arXiv:2312.13526](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.13526)

### Inferences
All [inference].

- Coverage formulation. Let cost(t | P) be the cheapest number of instructions to build target t from pool P (as estimated by our existing meet-in-the-middle or reduction search). Pool selection is: choose P with |P| ≤ 48 − (slots needed as scratch) minimising Σ_t cost(t | P) + (instructions to build P). Greedy by marginal benefit per instruction is the view-selection algorithm. Its 63% guarantee does **not** carry over: our benefit is not submodular, because two values can be useless alone and valuable together (t = a·b + c). Mitigation used in that literature: evaluate pairs or small bundles as single candidates.
- Time-varying pool. The pool is not one set but a sequence. Model each candidate value as an interval [creation, last use] with weight = instructions saved by having it over rebuilding or doing without it. Choosing which intervals to keep so that at most 48 overlap is the weighted interval problem that is polynomial (section 3). This gives an exact answer to "which values stay alive" for a fixed target order and fixed set of candidate uses, and it is the piece our current eviction rule approximates.
- Interleaving with target order. Targets that draw on the same intermediates should be contiguous, so the intermediate's interval is short. Formally this is a linear-arrangement problem on the hypergraph "intermediate → set of targets using it", minimising the maximum number of open hyperedges (the same layout problem that Austrin et al. show equals one-shot pebbling). Practical heuristics: DFS over the sharing structure, or local search on the target permutation with the interval solver as the evaluator.
- Why this differs from what we tried. "Shared intermediates first" decides *what* to build early; it does not decide when to let go. Large-neighbourhood search with a feasibility test treats registers as a constraint; the formulations above treat a register slot as a priced resource, which lets a move that frees a slot be credited even when it adds an instruction.
- Expected benefit. Bounded by the pool-size table in section 2: if better retention makes the effective pool behave like a pool 1.5–2 times larger, the counting bound moves by about 0.4–0.8 instruction per target; realised gains will be a fraction of that. On a 147,310 total, even 0.1 instruction per target is about 2,700 instructions.
- Reasons it might not transfer. cost(t | P) is itself the output of a heuristic search, so the "weights" are noisy; non-submodularity can make greedy pool choices poor; and a pool tuned to one target order can be wrong for another, so the order search and the retention solve must be iterated.

### Gaps
- No source formulates pool retention for arithmetic straight-line programs as coverage or submodular maximisation; the mapping is mine.
- The original Harinarayan–Rajaraman–Ullman paper could not be fetched; the 63% statement is from a secondary summary.
- I did not find literature on caching with *look-ahead where requests depend on cache content* (our case: what we choose to build depends on what is alive). Standard offline caching assumes a fixed request sequence.
- Data-pipeline work on choosing which intermediate results to materialize under a budget (e.g. reuse optimisers for ML workflows) was not searched; it may contain closer analogues.

## 5. Targets need only appear once: strategies that exploit "output once, then discard"

### Takeaway
The classical black pebble game already has this rule ("each output vertex must be pebbled at least once"), so every time–space result in Savage's chapter is for our semantics; the literature that keeps outputs pebbled (reversible pebbling, red-blue pebbling, some complexity papers) is the stricter variant and overstates our register need. I found no paper devoted to exploiting transient outputs as a technique; what exists are worked examples (FFT pebbling) and the slide move.

### Cited Findings
- [V] Savage: "A pebble can be removed from a vertex, including an output vertex, at any time after it has been pebbled" and "(Goal) Each output vertex must be pebbled at least once." — [Savage ch. 10, §10.1](https://cs.brown.edu/people/jsavage/book/pdfs/ModelsOfComputation_Chapter10.pdf)
- [V] Same convention: the aim is "that eventually all outputs get pebbled at least once". — [van Emde Boas & van Leeuwen](https://perso.ens-lyon.fr/loris.marchal/docs-data-aware/papers/pebble-shift.pdf)
- [V] Worked example: the FFT graph on n = 2^k inputs has n outputs but "can be pebbled with no more pebbles than are needed to pebble the trees with n leaves contained within it, namely, k + 1"; outputs are produced one or two at a time and their pebbles reused, at the price that "inputs must be repebbled". — [Savage ch. 10, §10.1.2](https://cs.brown.edu/people/jsavage/book/pdfs/ModelsOfComputation_Chapter10.pdf)
- [V] Stricter variants that require outputs to hold pebbles at the end: Demaine & Liu define the final configuration as exactly the target set; Meuli et al. require "at time K all the outputs need to be pebbled"; red-blue pebbling requires every sink to carry a pebble in the final state. — [Demaine & Liu, arXiv:1707.06343](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06343); [Meuli et al., arXiv:1904.02121](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.02121); [Papp & Wattenhofer, arXiv:2005.08609](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.08609)
- [U] Terminology: a *visiting* pebbling has each sink pebbled at some time; a *persistent* pebbling ends with pebbles on all sinks; the two prices can differ. — search summary of [Nordström, arXiv:1307.3913](https://arxiv.org/abs/1307.3913) and [arXiv:1003.3047](https://arxiv.org/abs/1003.3047) (not located in the text I extracted)
- [V] The slide move (result written over an operand) saves exactly one pebble. — [van Emde Boas & van Leeuwen](https://perso.ens-lyon.fr/loris.marchal/docs-data-aware/papers/pebble-shift.pdf)
- [V] In Chen's min-register model a value is live at a step only if one of its uses is scheduled later (the live bit is an OR over its uses), so a value with no uses adds nothing to register pressure. — [Chen, arXiv:2303.06855](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06855)
- [V] "Persistent" pebbling cost (pebbles must remain on the target at the end) is used as a separate quantity from the ordinary cost in the black-white results on trees and pyramids. — [Demaine & Liu, arXiv:1707.06343](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06343)

### Inferences
All [inference].

- A target that is never used as an operand has zero live range. It needs a destination register only for the instruction that produces it; that destination can be (a) one of its two operands if that operand is dead afterwards (slide), or (b) any register holding a dead value. If both operands stay alive and all 48 registers hold live values, one register must be free: the usable pool is then 47 plus one scratch slot. Budget the pool as 47, not 48, unless slides are available.
- Consequence for ordering: among targets that are leaves, prefer to emit a target at the moment it is the *last* use of one of its operands; then it costs no slot at all and frees one. This is the "RP-reduction" rule of Chen applied to targets.
- Consequence for tools: when borrowing SAT/ILP encodings from reversible or red-blue pebbling, drop the final-state clause and replace it by "for each target, OR over steps of (computed at step i)". Otherwise the solver will reserve up to 30 registers for outputs, which is most of our 48.
- Targets as stepping stones: a target that is also an operand for other targets (near-equal targets, a target dividing another) behaves like any intermediate and should be scored by the interval model of section 4; its "free" status only applies after its last use.
- Starting values are the opposite of transient: unlike textbook inputs they cannot be re-placed for free, so each starting value that is needed late pins a register for the whole program. With the shadow price of section 2 (about 0.7 instruction per slot per puzzle, upper bound), it can pay to spend one instruction early to fold a rarely used starting value into something more useful, or to schedule all its uses first.
- Expected benefit: small per puzzle (one or two effective slots), but it is free to implement and it removes a systematic bias if any borrowed solver or our own feasibility test counts targets as live.
- Reasons it might not transfer: none structural; the only risk is double counting if our current register accounting already frees dead targets immediately.

### Gaps
- No publication was found that studies transient outputs as an optimisation lever (for example, bounds on visiting versus persistent pebbling price for multi-output DAGs with numbers we could use).
- The exact visiting/persistent definitions were not confirmed in a primary text during this session (only the use of "persistent" cost in Demaine & Liu was seen).
- Savage's FFT example relies on free re-pebbling of inputs, which the contest does not allow; I did not find an analysis of the multi-output case with non-replaceable inputs.
